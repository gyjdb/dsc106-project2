“China is beating the U.S. in the tech race.”

    For the proposition

    C1 and C2 argue that China is pulling ahead in growth and global share.

    Design decisions for C1

    1. −1.5

      Showing growth rates instead of totals

      We compared how quickly each country grew rather than how much it produced. This puts China ahead in all five categories and makes its progress look convincing. However, a country starting with a small amount can have a high growth rate while still trailing in total output. We considered showing totals beside the growth rates, but chose growth alone because it highlights China's progress and draws less attention to the gaps that remain.

    2. −1

      Turning IP royalties into a strength for China

      IP royalties are payments for using intellectual property, such as patents or software. China's 26% growth rate creates the tallest bar, making this category look like strong evidence for China. Yet the U.S. earned about $114 billion in 2020 compared with China's $8.6 billion, roughly 13 times as much. We considered adding those dollar totals, but kept the growth comparison because it supports our argument. This makes China's progress clear while hiding how far it still trails in earnings.

    3. +1.5

      Building trust with clear labels and a source

      A zero-based axis, labels on every bar, and a World Bank source make the chart easy to read and check. These are honest design choices that help readers trust the numbers. However, an accurate chart can still give an incomplete picture when it only shows growth rates. We considered removing labels for a cleaner look, but kept them because they make the comparison easier to follow and give our argument more credibility.

    4. −0.5

      Using different time periods

      Most indicators begin in 2000, while high-tech exports begin in 2007. Including exports adds another category where China leads in growth, but the shared subtitle can make readers assume all bars cover the same years. The footnote explains the exception, though it is easy to overlook. We considered starting every indicator in 2007, but kept the longer records where available. That preserves more data, although the different periods make the comparisons less consistent.

    5. −1.5

      Combining a broad title with bold Chinese labels

      The title says “every measure of innovation,” which turns five selected indicators into a claim about the entire tech race. Bold labels on China's bars also draw attention to its larger growth rates first. These choices create a clear takeaway, but the title goes beyond what five measures can prove. We considered “China grew faster on five selected indicators” with equal-weight labels. We chose the stronger title and bold Chinese values because they make the lead feel more complete.

    Design decisions for C2

    1. −1

      Comparing shares of the world total

      Showing each country's share of the world total makes China's larger output look like evidence of global leadership. Percentages also make the three comparisons easy to read together. However, larger totals can partly reflect a country's size rather than how much it produces per person. We considered comparisons per person or research spending relative to the size of each economy. We chose world shares because they emphasize China's role in worldwide production.

    2. −1

      Choosing a favorable year

      We chose 2018, China's peak year for patent applications in this dataset. The subtitle gives another reason: it is the latest year with complete world data. A common year makes the comparisons consistent, but that explanation leaves out how favorable the year is for China. We considered showing trends across several years. We kept 2018 for its complete coverage and strong pro-China comparison, although a single year cannot show whether the lead is lasting.

    3. −1.5

      Leaving out measures where the U.S. leads

      Patents, high-tech exports and research papers all show China ahead, so each comparison reinforces the same message. We left out IP royalties and researchers per million people, where the U.S. leads. This keeps the chart focused, but makes China's advantage look more widespread than a broader comparison would suggest. We considered including those U.S. strengths, but kept the selected three because counterexamples would weaken our argument that China is winning the tech race.

    4. −0.5

      Putting the largest gap first

      Patent applications appear first, with China at 61% of the world total and the U.S. at 12%. That large opening gap shapes the reader's first impression before the closer research-paper comparison at 21% versus 17%. The order makes China's lead feel decisive, although the labels still show that its advantage varies. We considered alphabetical order or starting with research papers, but chose patents first because the largest gap gives our argument the strongest opening.

    5. −1.5

      Treating higher quantity as technological leadership

      The title describes a larger share of “the world's technology,” but patent and paper counts do not tell us how useful the inventions or research are. Export value also does not show where the technology was invented. The bars show the chosen amounts clearly, yet the title encourages a broader conclusion. We considered measures of research impact, such as how often other studies cite a paper, or a title naming only the three indicators. We kept the broad title because it makes larger quantities sound like overall technological leadership.

    Against the proposition

    U1 and U2 argue that the U.S. still leads in IP income and research intensity.

    Design decisions for U1

    1. −1

      Picking IP income as the battleground

      Charges for the use of intellectual property give the U.S. a 13× lead, and C1 uses the same series, so this chart reads as a direct rebuttal. The catch is that the indicator bundles patents with copyrights (film, music, software), trademarks and franchise fees, so the U.S. lead is not purely technological. We considered resident patent applications and high-tech exports instead, but China leads both (about 4.4× in 2019 and 5.3× in 2020), which would have undercut our side.

    2. +0.5

      Absolute dollars on a linear, zero-based scale

      Plotting raw dollars on a conventional zero-based axis is the most literal encoding and lets readers check every value. It is also what flattens China's story: its IP income grew about 106-fold, from $0.08B to $8.6B, yet on this scale the red line barely leaves the floor. A log scale or an index (2000 = 100) would have shown China climbing far faster than the U.S., and a ratio chart would show the U.S. edge collapsing from 541× to 13×; we rejected all three because they argue the other side. We scored this slightly earnest because nothing is distorted: the persuasion comes from what a linear scale emphasizes.

    3. −1

      Calculating and shading the “lead”

      We added a calculated field, U.S. minus China, and made it the visual subject: the gap between the lines is shaded and bracketed at both ends (“$43B lead” in 2000, “$105B lead” in 2020). An absolute gap can keep widening even while the trailing country catches up quickly, which is exactly what happened here. Bracketing only 2000 and 2020 also hides that the gap peaked at $115.7B in 2014 and has shrunk every year since 2017. We considered a third bracket at the 2014 peak but dropped it because it would draw attention to that recent narrowing.

    4. −1.5

      A calm title that rests on nominal dollars

      “America's IP income lead has more than doubled since 2000” states a calculated fact ($43.4B to $105.5B is a 2.4× increase) in neutral wording, with no “dominates” or “crushes,” so it reads like reporting rather than advocacy. But it relies on current dollars: U.S. consumer prices rose about 50% from 2000 to 2020, so in real terms the lead grew by roughly 60%, not “more than doubled.” The subtitle does say “current US$,” which keeps the chart technically accurate while counting on readers not to do the inflation math. Our earlier title, “America earns 13 times China's IP income,” was more defensible but invited the question of how fast that multiple is falling.

    5. +1.5

      Earnest scaffolding: labels, definitions, source

      End-of-line labels ($114B, $8.6B) replace a legend, the subtitle defines the measure in plain words, and the World Bank source and snapshot date are cited. The $43B and $105B lead annotations and the 13× comparison give readers concrete takeaways. These choices are earnest and make the chart quick to read, but they also lend credibility to the framing choices above. Matching C1's colors and typography lets the charts be read side by side.
